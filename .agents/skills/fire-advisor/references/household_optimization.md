# Household, Spousal & Survivor F.I.R.E. Planning Reference

In the UK, the tax system treats individuals separately, not as joint filing units. Consequently, optimizing across two partners can double tax shelters and reduce lifetime household tax liabilities by tens of thousands of pounds. Crucially, realistic planning must also model the financial shock when the first partner dies.

---

## 1. Double Allowances Architecture

Every tax year, a married couple or civil partners have access to:

| Allowance | Per Person | Combined Household |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Personal Allowance** (0% Income Tax) | £12,570 | **£25,140** |
| **Basic Rate Tax Band** (20% Income Tax) | Up to £50,270 | **Up to £100,540** |
| **Stocks & Shares ISA** | £20,000 | **£40,000 / year** |
| **Pension Annual Allowance** | £60,000 | **£120,000 / year** |
| **Capital Gains Tax Exemption** | £3,000 | **£6,000 / year** |
| **Dividend Allowance** | £500 | **£1,000 / year** |
| **IHT Nil-Rate Bands (NRB + RNRB)** | £500,000 | **£1,000,000** (transferable) |

## 2. Equalizing Pension Pots for Decumulation

### The Unequal Pot Trap
Consider a couple requiring £50,000/year gross pension drawdown in retirement:
* **Scenario A (Unequal Pots)**:
  * Partner A has a £1.2M pot and draws the full £50,000.
  * Partner B has £0 pot and draws £0.
  * *Tax result*:
    * 25% tax-free PCLS = £12,500.
    * Remaining £37,500: £12,570 tax-free (Personal Allowance), £24,930 taxed at 20% = £4,986 tax.
    * Partner B's £12,570 Personal Allowance is **completely wasted**.
* **Scenario B (Equalized Pots)**:
  * Partner A draws £25,000.
  * Partner B draws £25,000.
  * *Tax result*:
    * Each takes £6,250 tax-free (PCLS).
    * Taxable portion = £18,750 each.
    * Personal Allowance (£12,570) deducted $\rightarrow$ only £6,180 taxed at 20% (£1,236 tax each).
    * Total household tax = **£2,472**.
  * **Net Savings**: **£2,514 per year** every single year in retirement just by having balanced pots!

## 3. The "First Death" & Survivor Shock (The Silent FIRE Killer)

Most simplistic retirement models assume partners die on the same day at age 90 or 95. In reality, one partner almost always predeceases the other, creating an acute structural financial contraction:

### The 4 Major Survivor Penalties
1. **Loss of One Personal Allowance**: The surviving partner drops from £25,140 of tax-free personal allowances to just £12,570. An additional £12,570 of pension drawdown is instantly dragged into the 20% tax net ($\approx £2,514/\text{yr}$ tax increase).
2. **Loss of One State Pension**: One full New State Pension (~£11,500/year in today's terms) completely vanishes. It does not transfer to the surviving spouse.
3. **Defined Benefit Pension Halving**: DB schemes (NHS, Teachers, Civil Service, private final salary) usually pay a survivor's pension of only **50%** of the member's entitlement.
4. **Non-Halving Household Overhead**:
   * Council tax single-person discount is only 25% (not 50%).
   * Home heating, broadband, building insurance, water, and property maintenance remain 100% unchanged.
   * Total essential living costs typically drop by only **10%–15%** (to 85–90% of former spend), while household income drops by **30%–50%**.

### Survivor Defense Protocol
* **Pots Re-keying**: Ensure wills and pension expression-of-wish forms designate the surviving spouse as beneficiary (spousal transfers are strictly exempt from IHT).
* **Pre-Funding Survivor Buffer**: Factor in that the survivor may live 10–20 years alone in a single-allowance tax bracket.
* **Avoid Over-Reliance on Single-Life Pensions**: Where available, evaluate joint-life annuity and pension survivor escalations.

## 4. Funding a Non-Earning or Lower-Earning Spouse

* **Relief Belongs to the Member, Not the Payer**:
  * A working partner can pay funds directly into a non-working partner's personal pension or SIPP.
  * Tax relief is granted based on the *member's* tax status.
* **The £3,600 Gross Limit**:
  * **Scenario-Based Mathematical Assessment & Explicit Exceptions**:
    1. **Personal Allowance Arbitrage (+25.0% Return)**: If the non-earning spouse will draw pension income within their £12,570 Personal Allowance in early retirement, the entire drawdown is 100% tax-free! Paying £2,880 net to withdraw £3,600 tax-free yields an immediate **+25.0% risk-free return**.
    2. **Basic Rate Parity (+6.25% Return)**: If drawn at the 20% basic rate, the 25% tax-free PCLS ensures an immediate +6.25% net gain.
    3. **Household Pot Equalization**: Building a dedicated pot for the non-earning partner enables the household to utilize **two Personal Allowances (£25,140/yr combined tax-free)** and **two Basic Rate bands (£100,540/yr combined at 20%)**, preventing the earning partner from bearing 40% higher-rate tax on a single concentrated pot.
    4. **When Caution Applies**: Only in the narrow circumstance where the non-earner *already* has substantial other guaranteed income (e.g., large DB pension or rental income) that completely consumes their Personal Allowance and pushes 100% of the DC withdrawals into the 40% higher-rate band does the math become dilutive (-12.5%). Even then, compare against directing the £2,880 into an ISA for tax-free compounding rather than imposing an unconditional prohibition.

## 5. Inter-Spousal Asset Transfers (Nil Gain / Nil Loss)

* Under Section 58 TCGA 1992, transfers between spouses or civil partners living together take place at **nil gain and nil loss**.
* **Practical Tactic**:
  * Transfer shares or unwrapped holdings with unrealized capital gains from the higher-earning partner to the lower-earning partner.
  * The lower-earning partner realizes gains within their own £3,000 CGT exemption and pays CGT at the basic rate (18% for shares vs. 24% for higher rate).
  * The proceeds are then split across both partners' £20,000 ISAs.
